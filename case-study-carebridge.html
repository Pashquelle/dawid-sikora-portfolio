A patient management system a small clinic could actually afford to use

Agyle CareBridge is a condition-agnostic care platform: one system meant to carry many long-term pathways. This case study is about the first pathway we took all the way down — a small clinic in England treating cerebral palsy, which was paying enterprise money for a hospital-sized PMS that had never heard of the way CP care actually runs. The job was to build the dedicated one: minimal enough to afford, complete enough to run a clinic on.

01 — The problem

Enterprise prices for a system that had never met this patient.

A small clinic in England treats children and adults with cerebral palsy. To run it, they were licensing a full hospital-grade patient management system — the kind sold to NHS trusts, priced in the millions a year, and sized for a building with a hundred departments in it.

The money was the visible problem. The fit was the real one. CP isn’t an episode that opens, gets treated and closes; it is a condition somebody lives in for decades, managed in small increments, with the household doing most of the work between appointments. None of that had a shape in a generic PMS. So the clinic paid a fortune for a system it then had to work around — spreadsheets beside it, notes outside it, the actual clinical picture assembled in somebody’s head before each appointment.

The simple version of this tool did not exist on the market, because nobody builds enterprise software for a clinic of this size. That gap was the brief: a dedicated PMS, minimal but genuinely functional, covering every requirement of working with CP patients and nothing else.

Minimal wasn’t the aesthetic. It was the price of entry — the clinic couldn’t afford a system it needed a department to operate.

02 — The platform and the pathway

CareBridge is condition-agnostic. We designed it through one condition anyway.

CareBridge is sold as condition-agnostic: one platform carrying neurodisability, frailty, musculoskeletal and mental health on the same rails. That’s a sales shape, not a design shape. „Works for any condition" is earned by making it work completely for one — generic-first fits nothing in particular and gets configured into a mess on arrival.

So cerebral palsy became the reference pathway. Every pattern had to survive a real caseload before it earned a place in the platform, and we logged which decisions were CP’s and which were everyone’s. The first pathway writes the rules for every pathway after it.

03 — How the work ran

One consultant, every week, from the first sticky note to the last screen.

Four designers on the project, and a standing weekly session with the clinic’s lead consultant. Not a sign-off meeting at the end of a phase — the same conversation at every stage, from the opening brainstorms through flows and wireframes to the final UI. We brought what we had, he said where it broke against a real Tuesday, and we took it back.

In healthcare that access is the whole game, and it is usually the thing you don’t get. On the NHS patient record work I led before this, validation ran through strategists and consultants who came back with consolidated feedback — useful, but it tells you what the organisation concluded, not where somebody hesitated. Here the hesitation happened in front of us, weekly, and it cost one meeting to fix instead of one release.

04 — The treatment view

Treatment was a tab. We made it a view onto one condition.

In the old PMS, treatment was a page of its own: a plan written months ago, with everything that happened since scattered across other tabs — appointments in one, medications in another, the family’s answers nowhere at all.

Here a treatment is a condition, and opening one pulls everything attached to it into a single view. Hydrocephalus carries its own related factors — the self-assessment domains that moved with it, scored and dated — its own appointments, each with the clinician, the document to bring and the recommendations left last time, and its own medications.

It is contextual rather than complete, which is the point. The same patient has other conditions; this view refuses to show them. Ask how the hydrocephalus is going and you get an answer about hydrocephalus, not a record to sift.

05 — The clinic side

One timeline, and a score you can open.

The clinic works in a browser: patients, messaging and documentation in one place, instead of one system plus the workarounds that grew around it. This is the screen a clinician opens before an appointment.

Comorbidities don’t take turns. They move together, and reading them one at a time reads as noise — so everything the clinic tracks sits on a single timeline instead of in separate views.

Every score on it can be opened. The numbers come from what families fill in between appointments, so a clinician can go from a summary straight to the answers behind it. Instead of “how has it been?”, the question becomes what happened in a particular week — and the record answers.

06 — The patient and family side

Assessments, specialists and paperwork, without the drive.

The second half of the system is a mobile app for parents, and sometimes for the patient alone — which is what set the target sizes and the one-decision-per-screen rhythm.

It carries the family’s half of the record: assessments between appointments, medication, the calendar, paperwork, and a direct line to the care team without going through reception.

That’s where the time came back. Much of the old flow was a journey that only moved paper — a drive across the county for something that was never clinical in the first place.

07 — Decisions

Three decisions, and what each one cost.

Each one was a trade, and I’d make all three again.

Search carries the navigation, not a menu tree

Why

A minimal system can’t afford deep menus, and a four-person clinic has nobody to learn one. So we put the effort into a contextual global search instead: one field that knows which patient is open and which part of the record you’re in, and reaches a document, an assessment or a medication without a path to memorise.

The trade-off

Search is excellent for returning to something and poor for discovering it. Anything people would never think to look for has to stay visible on screen anyway — and the ranking wants tuning against real use we only had from one clinic.

Minimal by subtraction, agreed every week

Why

Each feature had to survive one question from the consultant: does this get used on a real Tuesday? Anything that only survived on „it would be nice" got cut, which is how the system stayed cheap to run and quick to learn.

The trade-off

We shipped a tool with visible edges. Some genuinely useful things were deferred, and phase 2 inherited a backlog of them rather than a blank sheet.

The family app is a data source, not a courtesy portal

Why

A clinic cannot observe a patient between appointments; the household can. Feeding routines and check-ins into the same record is what turns the app from a nice extra into the thing the clinical picture is built from.

The trade-off

The record is only as good as the logging habit, and a mobile screen now carries clinical weight it would never carry in a consumer app. A missed week is a gap in the data, not a lapsed streak.

08 — Where it got to

Phase 1 went in. I didn’t get to see phase 2.

The clinic went live on phase 1: the patient management core, the clinic workspace and the family app, replacing a system that had been costing them more in a year than this one will cost in several. Phase 2 — deeper clinical tooling and the wider rollout — was scoped for the end of 2026. I left the company at the start of Q2 2026, so I handed this over rather than shipped it, and I am not going to claim outcomes I wasn’t there to measure.

What I can claim is the shape of what went in: a clinic this size now runs on software it chose, instead of software it tolerated and paid enterprise rates for.

What I’d do differently. One thing. I would have put the family app in front of actual parents far earlier — we had excellent weekly access to a clinician and almost none to the people doing the daily logging, which is exactly backwards for the half of the product that lives in a household.
