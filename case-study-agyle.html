A huge, data-heavy healthcare ecosystem — for people who are counting seconds

Agyle Healthcare is a modular Electronic Patient Record running in NHS hospitals in the UK. Masses of data, everything connected to everything, and hospitals still working off paper while treating patients. The job was to digitise that flow and cut time on task on the interactions a nurse or a clinician repeats hundreds of times a shift. At Fortrus I led UX/UI for most of that work: new modules, time-on-task explorations, and the design system underneath it.

Module 01

Agyle Whiteboard — The whole Emergency Department on one screen, legible from across the room.

The live picture of an Emergency Department: who is where, what has been done, what is waiting, and whose job it is next. It is the ED Acute part of Agyle’s Urgent & Emergency Care suite, running in Dorset.

The same view runs on a tablet in one person’s hands and on a wall screen read by a room. So the first question was never „does it look good" — it was „can a nurse read it from six metres away while walking".

The board is a map of the department, not a list: zones, siderooms and infections sit where they sit in the building. The Holding column holds patients who arrived with no bed yet; you move one by dragging the tile onto a bay.

One tile, three densities

The map could be arranged more than one way, and zoom was the one that did the most work. Pulled back, a tile is a bed number, an acuity score and a clock — enough to count free beds and spot the one that has been waiting too long, from six metres away. One step in, it adds the patient, the clinician and the treatment timer. All the way in, it carries the diagnosis, the alerts, the notes and the flags.

Under the tile: the whole patient journey

The board was only the surface. Open a tile and Whiteboard has to carry a patient the entire way: arrival, triage, treatment, every handover in between, and discharge — each step leaving a trace somebody else can act on.

Tasks are the engine of that. A patient’s work sits in columns by status, each card carrying who asked for it, when, and how long it has been waiting. The same queue follows the staff onto a phone, because part of this job happens at the door and not at a desk.

So we set UX standards for Agyle rather than drawing each screen on its own: how a task is assigned, how a state is changed, how a record is opened and left. Staff learn one behaviour and get it everywhere — the only honest way to make a system this large faster to work in, not merely more complete.

Three decisions I made

One tile at three densities, not three screens

Why

A wall screen and a tablet needed the same board, and a separate layout per context would have drifted apart within two releases. Zoom carries the difference instead, so there is one thing to maintain and one thing to learn.

Keep the department’s geography

Why

A sorted list is easier to build and useless at a glance. Staff already navigate by where things are, so zones, siderooms and infections sit on the board where they sit in the building — you find a patient by looking, not by reading.

One panel for anything touching more than one patient

Why

Filters, escalation, admittance and audit could have lived next to the thing they act on. On a screen shared by a room, that means hunting between bays and mis-clicking into the wrong patient’s record. One known place is slower to reach and much harder to get wrong.

Module 02

Agyle Dashboards — two hospitals, one screen, and a reason to stop guessing.

An NHS Trust running two hospital sites already had dashboards. They reported everything and showed nothing: working out what was happening to patient flow right now meant reading numbers off a screen and comparing them in your head, between two other jobs.

So the charts and the summaries got rebuilt around the question actually being asked — what is happening now, and is it getting worse. Both sites sit inside every tile, one colour each all the way through, so comparison is the default reading instead of something you assemble from two tabs.

Almost everything here is measured in time: longest bed wait, average diagnosis time, four- and twelve-hour breaches. A number like that is worthless a shift late, so each one carries its trend next to it — the figure says where you are, the line says which way you are heading.

Three decisions I made

Both sites inside every tile

Why

The obvious build is one dashboard per hospital and a switch between them. That turns every comparison into a memory test. Putting both sites in the same tile makes the comparison the thing you read, not the thing you reconstruct.

Twenty charts to choose from, not one fixed screen

Why

A layout we decide on is a layout the Trust has to come back to us to change, and then wait for a release. So we handed over a set of twenty chart and summary types they arrange themselves — the dashboard follows whoever is reading it, ops, clinical or executive, without a ticket and without us.

No time figure without its trend

Why

Longest bed wait, diagnosis time, breaches — a time on its own tells you where you are and nothing about whether it is getting worse. Every one of them carries a line or an arrow next to it, because the direction is what anyone actually acts on.

Module 03

Agyle Scheduler — a day of parallel clinics, where a free slot is something you see rather than something you search for.

A standalone module for BARS and outpatient scheduling: who is booked, with which clinician, into which slot, across every clinic running that day.

The interface is swimlanes. Each clinic is a column with its consultant and its opening hours, time runs down the side, and a line marks where the day currently is. Hatching covers what is outside a clinic’s hours. A gap in a lane is therefore a free slot, drawn as empty space rather than stated as a number somewhere else — which is the whole reason for the layout.

Booking an appointment is the easy half. The job is everything after: moving one, cancelling one, removing it, locking a slot so nobody takes it, opening a new session when a clinic needs more room. An outpatient appointment is booked once and changed four times, so the module was built around the changing rather than around the booking.

The same day, zoomed out to a month

Swimlanes answer „what does today look like". They are useless for „when is there room in three weeks" — so the board switches to a calendar, and a month of load reads in one pass.

Every day carries two counts, patients and sessions, with a coloured edge for how hard it is being pushed. You are not reading dates; you are looking for the green ones.

A session is a rule, not an appointment

Nothing on the board exists until somebody opens a clinic, and that turned out to be the hardest form in the module. A session is not one event: it is which days of the week, from when, repeating how often, with what hours — and often different hours on a Monday than on a Wednesday.

So the form grows only as you commit to it. Tick different times per day and the per-day blocks appear; say the sessions repeat and the interval appears; leave custom intervals off and that half stays empty. What has to be filled in to submit is stated at the top, before you start, rather than in red after you have tried.

Three decisions I made

Swimlanes, not a list of bookings

Why

A list can only tell you what is booked. The thing a scheduler is actually hunting for is what is not — and a free slot has no row of its own. Laid out as lanes against a clock, availability becomes the empty space on the screen, and you stop searching for an absence.

The same global panel as the Whiteboard

Why

Scheduler is a separate module built by a separate squad, and it would have been easier to invent its own home for New Slot, Lock Slot and Assign. It uses the Whiteboard’s pattern instead: anything acting beyond the thing under your finger lives in one panel, in the same corner, in every module. That is what the design system was for.

Skip the empty days, but make it a switch

Why

Most clinics do not run most days, so stepping through the calendar one day at a time means tapping through emptiness. Smart Day View jumps to the next day that has something in it. It is a toggle, not a rule, because a scheduler sometimes needs to see the empty day on purpose — that is where a new session goes.

Module 04

Agyle Flow — finding a patient the right bed, with the reason it is the right one on the card.

Flow is capacity management: which beds exist across the wards and sites, which are occupied, which are being cleaned, which are isolation-ready, and who is waiting for one. It replaces the phone calls and the paper that hospitals ran this on, and a discharge here triggers the cleaning job that makes the bed real again.

Availability is the easy half of the question. A bed is only the right bed if it also matches the specialty, the gender mix of the bay, the side room requirement, the infection status and the equipment at the bedside — and all of that lives in systems a bed manager used to open one at a time. Flow pulls it onto one screen so the decision is made where the information is.

None of it is leisurely. Every request runs against a clock that is visible the whole time, because a patient waiting for a bed is a patient occupying something else — usually a trolley in the Emergency Department.

The system proposes, the bed manager decides

Automatic allocation ranks every candidate bed against three named criteria — gender, specialty, availability — and prints the result on the card as three ticks or 2/3 with the failing one marked. The beds that do not match are not hidden; they are ranked lower with their reason showing.

That was deliberate. A bed manager answers for the placement afterwards, and a list that just says „best" is a list they will second-guess by opening four other systems. Given the reason, they either accept it in a second or override it knowingly — and the patient’s own flags stay pinned on the left the whole time, because the things that make a bed wrong should never be on a screen you have left.

Three decisions I made

Show the misses, don’t hide them

Why

The easy build ranks beds and shows the good ones. But a bed manager is accountable for the placement, and „the system said so" is not a defence. Every card names which of the three criteria it fails, so an override is an informed choice rather than a fight with the software.

Three ways to allocate, one request

Why

Automatic is right when the ward is ordinary, manual when somebody knows something the data does not, and the map when the question is really about where in the building. Same bed request underneath all three — the method changes, the record does not.

The patient’s flags never leave the screen

Why

Infection status, a side room requirement, a violence warning: these are exactly the facts that make a bed wrong, and they used to sit in a different system. Pinned to the left of the chooser, they are in view at the moment of the decision rather than one screen behind it.

Module 05

Agyle UCR 2.0 — years of a patient’s paperwork, filed by the episode of care it belongs to.

UCR is where the documents live: scans, letters, reports, results — everything written about a patient across every episode and spell of care, which for anyone with a long history is thousands of files and a decade of them.

A flat list of that is a filing cabinet tipped on the floor. So the record is grouped the way clinicians already think about it: by spell, each one dated and counted, with its documents laid out as thumbnails underneath and tagged by the specialty that produced them. You narrow by episode first and by specialty second, which is how somebody asks the question out loud.

This was also the module where AI earned its place rather than being announced: an algorithm reads what is inside a document instead of trusting where it was filed, and automated summaries say what is in one before you open it. On a record this size, the value is not reading faster — it is knowing which five documents out of two thousand are the ones to read at all.

Smart Catalogues: filed by what the document says

A folder records where somebody put a file. It says nothing about what is in it, and in a record of two and a half thousand documents that is the only thing worth knowing. So instead of a better folder tree we built a different idea of filing: an AI pass reads each document and catalogues it by its clinical content — conditions, clinical conclusions, medications, allergies, contraindications.

Pick one of those and the record regroups around it. Choose Conditions and the list stops being files by date and becomes Pneumonia, Asthma, each with its own dates and its own documents underneath. The counter in the corner says 29 of 2562 — and those twenty-nine were chosen by what they contain, not by where a clerk once dropped them.

Three decisions I made

Group by spell, not by folder

Why

Folders are how a system stores things; episodes of care are how a clinician remembers them. „The inpatient stay in January" is a question somebody actually asks, and „the Radiology folder" is not — so the dated spell became the top level and specialty became a tag inside it.

Read and browse at the same time

Why

Opening a document used to mean losing the list. On a record with hundreds of files, that turns comparing two reports into a navigation exercise. The viewer sits beside the list instead of replacing it, so your place in the record survives reading.

AI catalogues and summarises — it never hides a document

Why

In a clinical record the dangerous failure is omission, not clutter. So the AI is allowed to read, catalogue and summarise — but narrowing the list is always something you switched on, labelled with the count you are seeing and cleared in one click. The model changes what you open first; it never quietly decides what you never see.

Module 06

Agyle Booked Admissions — planned surgery, booked against a theatre list and a ward bed at the same time.

The most complex thing in the suite. Booked Admissions schedules planned inpatient surgery: a full calendar-based tool where clinical teams book a patient into the right surgical slot on a ward that will still have a bed for them when they wake up. It runs the admission end to end — not just the moment it is entered into the diary.

What makes it hard is that a booking has to be true in several places at once. A slot is only real if the theatre time, the surgeon, the ward capacity and the patient’s own pathway all agree on it — and each of those is maintained by a different team with a different idea of what the day looks like. The calendar had to hold all four without turning into a spreadsheet.

The point of the module is theatre utilisation. An empty theatre slot is one of the most expensive things a hospital owns, and a cancelled admission on the morning of surgery wastes it at the last moment anybody could have filled it. So the work was less about booking and more about making the gaps, the clashes and the fragile bookings visible early enough to do something about them.

A month of theatre time, as a heat map

The four-day view answers „can I put this patient in on Thursday". It cannot answer the question a waiting list actually asks: where, in the next two months, is there room at all. So the calendar zooms out to whole months and stops printing bookings — each day becomes a single tile carrying one number, the theatre minutes still unsold, with the colour reading that number rather than a status.

It also makes the uncomfortable thing visible. The scheduler is hunting for the emptiest days, because that is where the next patient fits — but a month of empty days is a month of theatre time nobody sold. The same screen serves the person booking and the person answering for utilisation, and it lets them disagree out loud.

Three decisions I made

One calendar, not one per team

Why

Theatres, wards and admissions each had their own view of the same day, which is how a patient ends up booked into a slot with nowhere to recover. Putting the booking on a single calendar made the conflict visible at the moment somebody creates it, instead of at the morning huddle.

Show the empty slot as loudly as the booked one

Why

Scheduling tools are built to display what is arranged, because that is what the data is. But the expensive thing here is the gap — unused theatre time nobody noticed in time to fill. The calendar reads for holes first, the same instinct as the Scheduler’s swimlanes.

A booking carries its own preconditions

Why

An admission is not confirmed by being in the diary; it depends on assessments, consent and a bed. Hanging those on the booking itself meant the calendar could show which admissions are solid and which are one missing step from collapsing — which is the difference between a plan and a wish.

What it taught me

Four years, and the honest parts are the awkward ones.

Working across parallel Agile projects meant adapting to whatever shape delivery took that quarter. It also had a cost worth naming: feedback loops stretched, and developers split across several streams of work lost continuity inside a delivery cycle. Design that assumes an undivided team is design that will be half-built.

Healthcare added the harder constraint. Direct access to end users was limited, so validation ran through Business Strategists, Technical Consultants and operational or managerial NHS staff, who reviewed and tested internally and came back with consolidated feedback. That works — those people know the domain cold — but it puts a step between design intent and a clinician actually using the thing on a shift. Consolidated feedback tells you what the organisation concluded; it does not tell you where somebody hesitated.

What I'd do differently. I would have fought harder, and earlier, for time on the floor. Even a handful of observed shifts in an ED — not interviews, just watching what people do with their hands while the department is busy — would have settled arguments we instead resolved in review meetings. The design system was the right call and I would make it again; the research access was the thing I accepted as fixed when it was only difficult.
