A holiday booking flow that survives the fifth step

BajeczneWakacje.pl could sell a dream in three seconds and then had no way to take the money — you reserved and waited for the office to ring back. And when a sale did land, it was often somebody else’s trip. The agency’s own offers, the higher-margin ones, were buried under third-party listings on its own site. I redesigned the whole path — search, offer, pre-booking, payment — including the screens nobody puts in a portfolio, and built the design system the site had never had.

01 — The problem

The dream sold itself. Then the site ran out of road.

You couldn’t buy a holiday on it — you could submit a reservation and wait for the office to ring you back. And getting as far as an offer was a round trip: click a tile, land on the offer page, click “check offer”, and wait for a pop-up to tell you whether the price you had just been reading still existed. People lost interest somewhere in that loop, and the ones who didn’t were handed to a phone queue.

Two more problems sat underneath. The agency’s own trips, the higher-margin ones, shared a list, a card and a visual weight with resold inventory, so customers routinely booked somebody else’s product on a site the agency pays for. And there was no design system — no shared type scale, no spacing rules, buttons that looked alike without being the same component. That is why the site felt dense, and why the funnel couldn’t be fixed page by page: nothing was holding the pages together. So the brief had three pieces, not one — the funnel, the offers, and the language the site had never had.

“I got to the last screen and the price wasn't the price any more. So I closed it.”

The same complaint, in different words, across the agency’s Google reviews.
02 — What I found

No research budget. So I used the evidence that was already lying around.

Nobody was paying to put parents of three in a lab, and there was no analytics access to argue with. What there was: a full UX/UI audit of the live site against the Baymard Institute’s e-commerce guidelines, the agency’s own Google reviews, and a long interview with the stakeholder who had watched this funnel for years.

That combination is worth naming honestly. Baymard tells you where a checkout breaks for people in general; Google reviews tell you which of those breaks this checkout’s customers actually got angry about; the stakeholder tells you what the business already suspected and couldn’t prove. None of it is a substitute for watching a real customer try to book. I flagged the findings below as what they are — well-grounded inference, not observation.

  1. People searched twice because they didn't believe the first result

    The form asked for six fields and then returned everything anyway. Nobody trusts a result they don't feel they narrowed down themselves.

  2. The site asked people to leave in order to find out anything

    Checking whether an offer was still real meant opening its page and triggering a pop-up that passed a verdict on it. Comparing three offers meant doing that three times, from scratch. And the path ended where it should have started paying off: a reservation form and a promise that somebody would call back. Every one of those is an exit the site builds for itself.

  3. The site hid its own best product

    Own trips and resold ones shared a list, a card and a visual weight. Nothing on the page said which was which, so the only thing sorting them was price — and the agency’s own offers are rarely the cheapest line in a list. The site was doing the comparison shopping for the customer, against itself.

03 — The system underneath

A booking can fall apart at every step, so I mapped the steps first.

I mapped the whole path and everything that can go wrong on it before drawing a single screen: what expires, what can change price, what can vanish, and what the customer is owed when it does. A hotel can sell the last room while a card is still processing, and the screens have to survive that. That map is what the five payment steps came out of — there was no checkout to inherit, so the sequence had to be argued for from that map rather than from whatever the integrations wanted first.

Mapping it also settled the question that had been blocking everything else. The old offer search was slow enough to be the experience — no amount of layout fixes a wait people won’t sit through. We had budget for it, so it got replaced rather than decorated, on an off-the-shelf search framework instead of something written from scratch. That is a less romantic answer than building one, and it was the right one: it bought the speed in weeks rather than months, and it moved the design work off “how do we make waiting bearable” and onto what a fast search actually lets you do.

04 — The search

“Anywhere” is a real answer, so the form accepts it.

The old search asked for a destination and a date before it would show you anything. That is backwards: plenty of people have a budget and a week off and no particular country in mind, and the form turned them away at the first field.

So the new one takes “anywhere” and “from anywhere” as values rather than placeholder text — pick them and you get offers immediately, from every airport to everywhere. The destination picker leads with popular regions instead of an empty text box, because a list you can recognise beats a box you have to fill, and each chosen country opens into its own regions when you want to go finer.

Everything else lives one click down, under “more options”: board, accommodation, hotel standard, named hotel, transfer, amenities, tour operator, price. Each filter carries a count of what you have picked, so the panel reports your criteria back to you without being opened — and nobody has to remember what they narrowed three steps ago.

05 — The offer card

The card answers the question you used to leave the page to ask.

The old flow made you go looking for proof that an offer was real. Click a tile, land on the offer page, click “check offer”, wait for a pop-up to rule on a price you had already decided to pay. Three screens of effort to answer one question, and the answer was often no.

Now the card answers it where you are. It carries the departure airport, the dates, the length of stay, the board, the room, the transfer, both flight legs with their times and flight numbers, the tour operator and the price per person. Availability and the current price are checked behind it, so what you are reading is what you can buy — which is why the button says “select offer” and not “check”.

06 — The calls I made

Six decisions that shaped the redesign.

Buy the search engine, don't build it

Why

The old search was too slow and people left before it answered. We replaced it with a ready-made search framework. Writing our own would have taken months the project didn't have.

Ask for less before answering

Why

The old form wanted six fields before it showed anything. Now you type a destination and get results straight away, then narrow them down with filters.

Results in the list, not behind a pop-up

Why

You used to open an offer and wait for a pop-up to tell you whether it still existed. Now results appear at the bottom of the search section and each card says if it's available.

The booking ends in a payment, not a phone call

Why

You could only reserve a trip and wait for the office to call back. Now you pay online, in five steps that are all shown up front and can all be reopened.

Own trips look like own trips

Why

The agency's own offers looked identical to resold ones, so people chose by price and the agency lost the better margin. Own offers now get a different card, a label and the featured spots.

The system before the pages

Why

There was no type scale, no spacing rules and no shared components, so every page looked slightly different. I built those first and the screens second.

07 — The checkout

Five steps, a running total, and nobody on the phone.

The old booking ended in a phone call. You picked a trip, filled in a form, and then a consultant told you what else you needed and what it would cost. That call was where add-ons were sold, and it was also where people dropped out — nobody wants to negotiate a holiday at 9am on a Tuesday. The new flow does the whole purchase without a consultant, add-ons included.

Two things carry that. The total price is always on screen and updates as you tick things on — so the number at the end is the number you have been watching, not a surprise. And every field that wasn’t strictly required got cut. Less to read, less to fill in, fewer places to give up.

08 — The design system

No type scale, no spacing rule, no shared button. So those came first.

The site had nothing holding its pages together. Every screen carried its own type sizes and its own margins, and the buttons looked related without being the same component. That is why the funnel couldn’t be repaired one screen at a time — there was no second screen to repair it against.

So the library was built before the screens. Colour with its roles written down rather than remembered, a type scale that runs from the biggest headline to the label inside a button, and one spacing rule every margin obeys. Then the components the booking actually needed, in every state — including the error and disabled ones that nobody draws until something breaks in production.

Every screen in this case study is assembled from it. That is the whole point: the next person to touch this site inherits a kit, not a set of screenshots to copy by eye.

09 — The result

A funnel that holds its price, and a site that stops hiding its own product.

↘

Cognitive load. Fewer fields before the first result, offers in one list instead of behind a pop-up, and a price that stays the same from the first screen to the last.

What shipped: an online checkout where there had been a reservation form and a phone number, results that appear in the list instead of behind a pop-up, five payment steps you can see and go back through, and the agency’s own trips given their own card and their own place on the page. Desktop and mobile.

Underneath it, a design system that hadn’t existed: a type scale for desktop and mobile, spacing and colour tokens, and components covering the states this product has — offer available, offer gone, payment pending, payment done. The next page somebody builds will look like it belongs here without asking me.

I don’t have post-launch numbers. I left before the data came back.

What I'd do differently. Every finding on this page came from an audit, a stakeholder and a pile of Google reviews. Not one of them came from watching a person try to book a holiday on this site. Baymard's guidelines are good and the reviews were specific, but both tell you about customers in aggregate and after the fact — neither tells you which of your five steps a particular person gives up on and why. Five sessions with real customers, run on the wireframes before any UI existed, would have cost a day and told me more than the audit did. I didn't push for it because there was no budget line and the audit felt like enough. It wasn't; it was just the part that was free.
